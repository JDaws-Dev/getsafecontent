I wanted to give Apple the benefit of the doubt.

When parents ask me "do I really need SafeTunes?", I tell them to try Apple's built-in filter first. If it works for your family, great. Save the money.

But I decided to actually test it myself. For 30 days, I turned on Apple Music's Content Restrictions (Settings > Screen Time > Content & Privacy > Music) and set it to "Clean."

Then I used the account like a curious 11-year-old would.

Here's what happened.

## What Apple's Filter Is Supposed to Do

Apple Music's Content Restrictions setting hides songs that are tagged as "Explicit" (the little red "E" badge). When you turn it on:

- Songs marked explicit are hidden from search results
- Explicit versions of albums are replaced with "Clean" versions when available
- Explicit songs can't be played, even with a direct link

Sounds reasonable. In theory.

## What Actually Got Through

### Week 1: The "Clean" Versions Problem

The biggest issue isn't what the filter blocks — it's what it allows.

A "Clean" version of a song is whatever the record label decides to submit. Usually they bleep or remove the most obvious profanity. But the song's *content* doesn't change.

Example: I found "Clean" versions of songs about:
- Detailed drug use and dealing
- Sexual encounters (described in everything but explicit words)
- Graphic violence (no profanity, but still violent imagery)
- Glorification of gang activity

The filter checked for bad words. The content was still completely inappropriate for kids.

### Week 2: The Missing Tags Problem

Not every song with inappropriate content is tagged "Explicit."

Record labels self-report the explicit tag. There's no verification. Smaller labels, independent artists, and international releases frequently skip the tag entirely.

I found dozens of songs with heavy profanity, drug references, and sexual content — all untagged, all fully available with the Clean filter on.

The filter can only block what's labeled. And a lot of inappropriate music isn't labeled.

### Week 3: The Album Artwork Problem

Even with the Clean filter on, album artwork is completely unfiltered.

This means your kid can be listening to perfectly clean music while staring at an album cover featuring:
- Suggestive or sexualized imagery
- Violent or disturbing artwork
- Drug-related imagery

Apple provides zero controls for album artwork. None. The filter doesn't even acknowledge it exists.

My 11-year-old asked me about an album cover she saw while browsing. I won't describe it here, but it was the reason I built SafeTunes.

### Week 4: The Discovery Problem

Here's the thing nobody talks about: even when the filter works perfectly, your kids can still *search* for anything.

They can search for any artist, any song, any album. They can read song titles. They can see album descriptions. The filter just prevents playback of tagged content.

So your 10-year-old can still type in the name of any artist they heard about at school, see all their albums, read all the song titles, and see all the artwork. They just can't press play on the ones marked explicit.

That's not parental control. That's a speed bump.

## The Numbers

After 30 days of testing, here's what I found:

| Category | Count |
|----------|-------|
| Songs with inappropriate content that passed the filter | 40+ |
| Albums with concerning artwork (no filter available) | 25+ |
| Artists with zero explicit tags despite explicit catalogs | 12 |
| "Clean" versions that still contained inappropriate themes | 30+ |

The Clean filter caught the most obvious stuff — songs with prominent profanity from major labels. Everything else got through.

## Why This Isn't Apple's Fault (Exactly)

Apple's filter does what it says: it hides content tagged as "Explicit." The problem is that the tagging system is broken:

1. **Labels self-report** — No verification, no standards
2. **"Explicit" only means profanity** — Not violence, not themes, not imagery
3. **International content is rarely tagged** — K-pop, Latin music, etc.
4. **Album art has no rating system at all** — Nobody even thought about it

Apple can't fix this without manually reviewing millions of songs. That's never going to happen.
